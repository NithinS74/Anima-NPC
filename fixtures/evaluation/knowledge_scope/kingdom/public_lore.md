# Harrowmere public lore

## Highkeep

Highkeep is the royal fortress of Harrowmere, built on a granite spur above the Ambry valley. Its outer walls were raised by King Oswin's great-grandfather after the Border Riots. Visitors may enter the lower bailey on market days, when the gates stay open from dawn to dusk. The upper keep is closed to all but the household and those summoned by the crown. Every autumn the king's stewards read the year's decrees from the bailey steps, and the crowd may present petitions to the clerks who sit beneath the gate arch.

## Roads and rivers

The Ambry river runs from the northern hills to the eastern marshes and marks much of the border with Vossari land. Two roads serve the east. The old river road follows the western bank and floods most springs. The king's road runs further inland and was paved during the last peace. Ferries operate at Millbrook and Dunmere, and merchants pay a toll at each. Bridges over the Ambry were few even before the war, and several wooden ones have since been burned by raiders from both sides.

## The war with the Vossari

The Vossari are a people of the eastern plains, led by a council of horse lords. The present war began two years ago when Vossari riders burned the toll house at Dunmere in a dispute over grazing rights. Harrowmere has lost two border towns and retaken one. Heralds announce victories in every market square, and the families of the dead receive a small grant of barley. Neither side has offered terms in public, and traders say the fighting has moved closer to the river this year.

## Taxes and coin

The crown collects a hearth tax each spring and a tithe of grain each autumn. In wartime the king may also levy special taxes, as earlier kings did during the Border Riots. Coin in Harrowmere is struck at the royal mint in Highkeep, and the silver mark is the common unit of account. Debts to the crown are recorded by district clerks. Farmers often pay in grain rather than coin, and clerks are widely distrusted for their weights and measures.

## The levy

Each village must send one in every twenty able men to the royal levy in times of war. Levy men serve for a single campaign season and are expected to return for the harvest, although many do not. They train with spear and shield under garrison sergeants. Garrisons are kept at Millbrook, Dunmere and the eastern towns, and their captains report to the crown through royal couriers. Armies in Harrowmere have traditionally marched in late spring, once the roads have dried.

## King Oswin

King Oswin came to the throne at nineteen and has ruled for thirty-one years. He is known for his love of falconry and for settling the Border Riots by marriage rather than war. His queen died eleven years ago, and his only son, Prince Edric, serves as warden of the north. In earlier years the king toured the valley towns every summer. Court poets praise his patience, while his critics call him slow to act. Portraits of him hang in most guild halls.

## Religion and festivals

The people of Harrowmere honour the harvest saints, whose shrines stand at the edge of most villages. The Feast of First Sheaves falls at the start of harvest and is the largest festival of the year. Priests bless the seed grain in spring and keep the birth and death rolls for each parish. During the war, the shrines have also collected bread for widows. Travelling preachers sometimes speak against the war, and the crown tolerates them so long as they do not name the king.

## Mercenaries and foreign trade

Companies of mercenaries have fought in the eastern wars for generations. They sell their service by the season and are known by their banners, such as the Red Hand and the Iron Bell. Harrowmere law forbids them to enter a walled town under arms. Foreign merchants bring salt, iron and dyed cloth up the Ambry, and salt in particular has grown costly since the war closed the eastern pans. Guilds in Dunmere have complained to the crown about the price of iron.
